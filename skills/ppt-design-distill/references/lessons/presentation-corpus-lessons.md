# Presentation corpus lessons

Read when choosing a deck's density, title system, navigation or page patterns,
or when interpreting a business presentation reference. These lessons come from
one private study. They are candidates to test, not rules of a new reference and
not a house style. Evidence from the current reference and brief takes priority.

## What was studied and how far it reaches

- 34 business presentations: 26 PPTX and 8 PDF, about 1,200 rendered pages. They
  cover promotion reviews, strategy reviews, organization and market research,
  investor and executive talks, customer exchanges, teaching and template kits.
- PPTX files were parsed for titles, geometry, declared styles, charts, tables,
  notes and transitions, then rendered. All pages were viewed on contact sheets;
  a few were enlarged. Contact sheets show structure, not small-text legibility.
- The sources are private and are not distributed. This file keeps relationships
  in our own words, without their content, names, figures, logos or page images.
- The collector labeled these decks high quality. No audience or business outcome
  was measured, so every stated effect below is an inference.
- Many decks share one organization's templates. Repetition across them is weak
  evidence: their shared blue palette, for example, is not a lesson about color.
- Rendering substituted fonts, so typeface character and tight text fit were not
  judged. Animation was detected in the files but not played.

## 1. Let the reading situation set density

Density followed how a deck is consumed more than its topic. Counts are each deck's
median characters per slide outside tables, so table-heavy decks read denser than
shown; one research deck that was mostly tables measured about 20 and is left out.

| Situation | Text outside tables | Job of a page | Typical proof |
|---|---|---|---|
| Executive or technical live talk | About 55-175 | Carry one spoken step | Statement, product image, demo still, one metric |
| Customer exchange | About 75-125 | Make the offer relevant to this audience | Case, architecture, customer result |
| Promotion or level review | About 140-470 | Show judgment and scope for evaluators | Timeline, result, self-assessment |
| Strategy pre-read or review | About 80-735 | Replace a document and support decisions | Target table, matrix, goal tree |
| Research or benchmark report | About 120-685 | Stand alone without a presenter | Comparison, org chart, cited source |
| Investor roadshow | About 155 | Tie market evidence to the plan | Market curve, policy marker, footnote |

Decide this first. The same 700-character page can suit a pre-read and fail on
stage. One file labeled for screen projection still carried very dense tables,
which suggests a label does not change density unless the pages are rewritten.

## 2. Choose a title system on purpose

Outside the template kits, parsed PPTX titles had per-deck medians of about 13-43
characters; titles not in placeholders were missed. Forms observed:

| Form | Use when | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| Full-sentence claim | The reader should get the argument from titles alone | Two lines at most in the samples; longer titles crowd the content |
| `Label | claim` | A long review needs both location and argument | The label must stay a stable vocabulary, or it adds noise |
| `Track - segment | claim` | Research spans a hierarchy of topics | Depth costs title width |
| Short topic | The presenter supplies the claim, or the page is a catalog entry | Self-read users lose the argument |
| Statement page | A spoken turn deserves the whole screen | Loses force if most pages use it |

Test by reading only the titles in order. If this yields an argument, the title
system works for a self-read deck. Topic titles can still be right for a keynote.

## 3. Keep the reader located across many pages

Long decks used visible position devices instead of relying on page numbers:

- **Agenda recap:** the same agenda returns with the current section highlighted.
  Works when sections are few and truly sequential; repeated too often it pads.
- **Question tracker:** an early page numbers the key questions, and later pages
  carry the number. Useful when a benchmark report answers fixed questions.
- **Progressive chips:** a statement repeats with its three challenge chips, each
  lit in turn before its evidence. Good for live talks with a countable structure.
- **Paged counters:** `(1/3)` in the title when one table cannot fit one page.
- **Question pages:** a sparse question sits between dense evidence pages, resetting
  attention in a long review. Multi-speaker decks add speaker and section dividers.
- **Marked appendix:** dividers, or a "backup" tag after the closing page, separate
  the argument from lookup material so skipped pages do not read as omissions.

## 4. Reuse a scaffold when items are peers

The most transferable mechanism was one layout repeated for items meant to be
compared: competitors, customer segments, markets or benchmark companies. Readers
learn the frame once and then see differences. Keep rows, order and field names
fixed, and write an explicit "not disclosed" or dash for gaps. Do not stretch this
to items that are not peers, since sameness then implies a false equivalence.

Recurring page patterns, each with its condition and cost:

| Pattern | Structure | Fits | Watch |
|---|---|---|---|
| Row-spine plan | Fixed left row labels, target band on top, columns per segment, quantified row at the foot | Planning across similar units | Tiny text; better as a pre-read |
| Peer scorecard | One column per competitor, same metric and summary rows, own side set apart | Fast competitive read | Mixed sources and dates need notes |
| Case triad | Summary, scene or product image, then 2-4 result numbers | Customer proof | Numbers need a baseline and scope |
| Layered stack | Horizontal layers, from foundation up to applications | Explaining a platform or architecture | Often lists components without an argument |
| Radial landscape | Concentric arcs from core capability out to scenarios | Showing breadth at a glance | Reads as texture; poor for exact lookup |
| Goal tree | Target at the top, branching into drivers and actions | Decomposing a number into work | Fixed levels only help if they are comparable |
| Heat table with trend | Units as rows, periods as columns, color for variance, small trend line | Many units over time | Needs a legend and a note on the threshold |
| Quadrant scatter | Two metrics, labeled units, regions named by meaning | Sorting units into actions | Labels collide; axes must be defined |
| Annotated curve | One time series with events, phases or policy marks placed on it | Explaining why a trend moved | Annotation must not imply unproven cause |
| Number rail | Line or curve in the main area, a column of key figures at the side | Big-picture context with precise support | Each figure needs a source note |
| Self-assessment grid | Evaluation criteria as cells, each with evidence | Reviews against stated criteria | Unsupported ratings read as boasting |
| Two-state reveal | The same page returns with an added recommendation | Challenge to response in order | Static export shows near-duplicate pages |

## 5. Assign emphasis a job

- Stronger decks used one main hue plus neutrals, with a second hue or red kept for
  exceptions, priorities or risks. Readers can then scan for what stands out.
- A 200-page two-color template kept focus, base and context roles across many
  diagram types. A rainbow infographic kit used hue only to separate items, so
  color carried no meaning. Replace kit colors with roles before using a kit.
- Dense consulting-style pages bolded a sentence-level skim path through the body,
  which lets one page serve both a fast and a careful reader.
- Status marks such as stars, check and cross icons, and colored totals work when
  defined on the page and used sparingly. Icons alone cannot hold a claim.

## 6. Make evidence checkable in proportion to the claim

- Source footers varied by genre: most pages of a benchmark report, about half in
  market research and roadshows, almost none in reviews or live talks. Count was
  measured by pattern and may miss sources placed elsewhere.
- A research deck stated that its figures were external and partly inferred. A
  one-line scope note protects both the claim and the speaker.
- Effective charts sorted categories, placed the takeaway on the chart, and used
  markers to tie a legend to a position. Do not reproduce an original chart's data
  or claims when transferring its form.
- Decks masked sensitive values with placeholders before sharing. Check that a mask
  is applied to the file itself, and that notes, hidden slides and embedded chart
  data do not retain the value.

## 7. Treat long decks as a modular library

One organization's 146-page master and 27-page short version shared whole pages.
A custom executive deck placed the customer's own case early, then reused the same
system. The reusable unit is the page with its scaffold, not the whole storyline:
choose and reorder for each audience, then rewrite the opening and close. Before
reuse, recheck that dated figures are still current, and that one customer's
material can be shown to another. The collector's notes flagged both concerns.

## 8. Permit deliberate departures

Some of the most memorable moments broke the system with a clear reason:

- One multi-speaker meeting deck switched to a paper-collage style for a culture
  section, then returned to its standard system for awards. The change marked a
  different kind of talk. Cost: production effort and a sharp style jump.
- A strategy talk used a "one more thing" page to reframe the next section as a
  new capability. It depends on delivery and may read as a gimmick in print.
- A dark full-bleed network page conveyed the idea of complexity; its labels were
  not meant to be read. Keep this as atmosphere, and put real evidence elsewhere.

Judge a departure by its intent and effect in context, as candidate design cases,
and compare with a plain alternative. See [type-and-creative.md](../method/type-and-creative.md).

## 9. Watch for these failure signs

These are risks seen in actual pages, not bans:

- A polished opening followed by pages of default bullets, suggesting that notes
  were pasted late. Audit finish across the whole sequence, not only the first pages.
- Glass panels, gradients and glowing diagrams that add surface but no hierarchy.
  Remove the effect mentally and see whether the argument still reads.
- Logo walls and ecosystem maps offered as proof. They show breadth, not outcomes.
- Text far below comfortable reading size on pages intended for projection.
- Template residue: placeholder headlines and sample labels left in a finished page.
- Infographic kits whose labels broke when real text replaced short samples. Font
  substitution may have contributed; test real text in the actual font.

## 10. Questions before using a lesson

1. How will this deck be consumed, and by whom? Does that justify this density?
2. If only the titles are read, what does the audience learn? Is that intended?
3. Where is the reader at any moment, and can they find their way back?
4. Are these items true peers? If so, is their frame consistent?
5. What does each color mean, and would removing it lose information?
6. Can the most important number be traced to its source, date and scope?
7. Does the new content actually have this structure, or is it being forced in?
8. What was tried as a plain alternative, and why was this choice kept?

Render the result and check it on actual pages. A pattern from this file proves
nothing about the new deck until it has been tested with the new content.
